# Audio

**The complete path.** The audio chain begins when the game writes samples into the speaker registers of the emulated remote. The console module interprets those writes, reads the header with the format, the sample rate and the volume, and accumulates the samples in one queue per player. Those queues are drained in transfers of at most 64 bytes, interleaved right after an input read completes, so that audio never makes input polling wait, which is the critical path for motion. On the adapter side, the samples are encoded in SBC at 16 kHz in packets of two frames and sent to the DualShock 4 speaker over Bluetooth. Playback is therefore handled by the controller, with the game audio coming out of the controller in the player hands.

**Why audio is aggregated.** The decision to accumulate samples instead of forwarding every write is aimed at bounding the number of USB transfers, which is the most delicate resource of the link. The game writes audio in small and frequent fragments; forwarding them one by one would multiply the number of transfers and compete with input polling. By aggregating, every transfer carries a useful block and the link usage remains bounded and predictable. The concrete numbers behind that decision are these: the game writes samples to the speaker registers in fragments of a few bytes at a time, the module accumulates them in one FIFO queue per player that holds up to 256 samples (with an emergency safety flush at 200 samples), and the queue is drained in transfers carrying up to 59 samples plus a five byte header, filling sixty-four bytes, which is the maximum payload of the USB interrupt endpoint. The adapter receives those blocks and encodes them in SBC at 16 kHz in packets of two frames, which is sixteen milliseconds of audio per report. The audio therefore travels the link as a few large blocks instead of many small ones, and the samples reach the speaker about sixteen milliseconds after they leave the game.

**Header changes.** When the format, the sample rate or the volume changes, the previous queue is drained first so that samples from different configurations are never mixed. If the change happens while a transfer is in flight, the old samples are dropped, because header changes in the middle of a stream are exceptional and carrying data that no longer corresponds is not worth it. A safety drain also exists, which activates when the queue approaches its capacity, so that a sustained burst cannot overflow it.

**Separation per player.** Each player keeps its own audio queue, and the player index travels in the header of every block. Two controllers can therefore play different audio at the same time without mixing samples, and the link recovery of one controller does not affect the stream of the other. Player one has priority over player two: the adapter cannot stream two audio channels at the same time, so the single transfer always carries player one's audio while it has any, and player two fills in otherwise. The audio is encoded at 16 kHz in SBC frames of 128 samples, so one frame covers 8 ms and a two frame report covers 16 ms; the source material is band limited to about 6 kHz, so the lower rate costs no audible quality.

**Limits and behaviour under failures.** Audio is failure tolerant by design: audio transfers use a dedicated interrupt OUT slot separate from input polling and control writes, ensuring audio and input never compete for link resources. If an audio transfer is delayed or an endpoint stalls, the hardware cancellation and watchdog retry the drain on subsequent cycles, so playback continues smoothly without affecting motion latency. The priority of the system is always input: under any link recovery or bandwidth pressure, audio yields. That hierarchy is deliberate and explains why audio takes part in no decision that could affect motion latency.

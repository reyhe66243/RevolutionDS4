# Limits

**What this document is.** Every project that reproduces one piece of hardware with another has a boundary: things that cannot be done, things that are deliberately not done, and things that work but are not identical to the original. This document states them plainly, with the reason behind each one. The reference is always the same: a real Wii Remote with a real MotionPlus, in the same game, doing the same gesture.

| Limit | Why it exists |
|---|---|
| The console's internal Bluetooth radio is not used | The 36 KiB budget, pairing logic and 16 kHz SBC audio encoding belong on the adapter |
| The Nunchuk is not supported | One inertial unit cannot describe two objects moving in two hands |
| No gyroscope pointer or joystick IR mode | The gyroscope drifts without an anchor; the touchpad preserves direct absolute pointing |
| The motion gain is a calibration | The in game deficit is measured and compensated; its mechanism is not identified |
| The console module has a 36 KiB budget | The loader accepts no more, and the margin is narrow |
| Maximum two controllers supported | Strict hardware and protocol ceiling: shared USB polling budget, radio airtime for 16 kHz SBC audio, and cIOS fake wiimote instances |
| The pointer comes from the touchpad | It is not an infrared camera and does not measure position in the room |

**Why the console's internal Bluetooth radio is not used.** Translating DualShock 4 data into Wii Remote data is not the difficult problem: the adapter already does that translation correctly. The problem is everything around it. The console's Bluetooth stack, its pairing model (the sync button and remote authentication), bond storage and reconnection logic all live inside the IOS module. That module has a hard 36 KiB budget and is the most delicate part of the system: if that code misbehaves, every controller connected to the console is affected, including real remotes. On the adapter, that work runs on a dedicated RP2040 without those constraints, and real remotes on the console remain untouched.

There is also a hard computational constraint: the speaker audio. The DualShock 4 speaker is driven by a 16 kHz SBC stream, and the encoding runs on the adapter's processor. Moving that inside the IOS module would put the encoding load on the console's processor, which has neither the CPU budget nor the memory space for it. Moving the Bluetooth host and audio encoding to the adapter places the heaviest, riskiest and most expensive tasks in the place designed to handle them.

**Controller scope: faithful 1:1 reproduction only.** The rule of the project is simple: everything that a single DualShock 4 can reproduce faithfully is in scope, and everything that cannot is not. The Classic Controller mode exists because the Classic Controller has no inertial unit: its sticks, buttons and triggers map one to one onto the DualShock 4. Attachments or modes that cannot be reproduced without inventing missing physical data are deliberately excluded.

**The Nunchuk cannot be emulated, and is not supported.** This is the deepest limit of the project and it is physical, not a matter of effort. A Nunchuk is a second controller with its own accelerometer, its own stick and its own buttons, and the game reads both units at the same time: the remote's motion and the Nunchuk's motion are two independent measurements of two independent objects held in two hands. A DualShock 4 has one inertial unit, so its samples describe the motion of exactly one object. Emulating both would require inventing the Nunchuk's motion from the same samples that already describe the remote's motion, and any invented motion is a lie that the game would believe and the player would feel.

Games that require the Nunchuk are still playable through compatibility patches that give the game Classic Controller support instead. Those patches exist as Riivolution patches or as ready made game patches; Super Mario Galaxy 1 and 2 have them, and collections cover other Nunchuk titles such as Donkey Kong Country Returns, Kirby's Return to Dream Land and New Super Mario Bros. Wii. With such a patch applied, the game reads the Classic Controller and the project's Classic Controller mode plays it with the DualShock 4. That is a patch to the game, not to this project: the project continues to refuse to invent motion data.

**The pointer is emulated from the touchpad, and does not use the gyroscope or joystick.** The real remote has an infrared camera that looks at the sensor bar and gives the game an absolute position in the room. The DualShock 4 has a capacitive touchpad, and the project maps it onto the infrared dot coordinates the game expects. The result is usable as an absolute pointer, but it has the touchpad's characteristics rather than an optical camera's.

A joystick-driven pointer is omitted because it lacks the physical intuition of pointing directly at a screen. Using the gyroscope as a pointer is also deliberately avoided: the console uses the sensor bar infrared dots as an absolute spatial reference to anchor pointing. A gyroscope has no absolute reference and accumulates drift over time; substituting it for infrared would drift continuously, requiring constant manual recentering right where pointing needs to be exact.

A physical consequence of this mapping: the pointer does not appear when the remote points straight up or straight down. In those orientations, gravity aligns along the length of the controller, leaving the horizontal gravity vector near zero. The console's tracking library checks whether the camera's IR sighting is consistent with the accelerometer's gravity vector; because nobody points at a television screen while holding a remote vertically towards the ceiling or floor, the console treats the reading as out-of-screen and suppresses the cursor.

**The gain calibration: 128.5 percent, and what is not explained.** The in game rotation was measured to produce about 78 percent of the physical turn with the DualShock 4, while a real remote with a MotionPlus produced a one to one correspondence in the same game and the same gesture. Every measurable stage of the chain was verified correct: the gyroscope integration of the controller is exact, the USB transport does not alter the values, the MotionPlus encoding matches the reference and the game reads a constant injected rate proportionally. The deficit was linear and uniform, and a single constant factor corrects it at all speeds and on all axes, so the compensation is applied in the console module and the physical data is left untouched upstream. The value, 128.5 percent, was tuned in game until a complete turn matched the gesture. The underlying mechanism was never identified: it is documented as an end to end calibration against the real hardware, not as a known error that was fixed. What is known is what is measured, and what is measured is that the game now rotates what the wrist rotates.

**Motion features that depend on the accelerometer magnitude are not verified end to end.** The accelerometer's scale and direction are verified and match the real remote, which is what tilt and steering use. Features that use the magnitude of the accelerometer instead of its direction — shakes, force gestures, throw strength — were not compared against the real remote in the same game. If a game ever needs more force than the real remote does, that is the place to look, and the same kind of gain calibration used for the gyroscope applies there.

**The console module has a hard size limit.** The IOS module (`REVOLUTIONDS4.app`) must strictly fit within **36,864 bytes (36 KiB / 0x9000)**. This is a hard physical ceiling imposed by the Starlet SRAM memory map in d2x cIOS (`0x13970000` to `0x13979000`), not a target or required size: any build `<= 36,864 bytes` is safe and valid. Growing past this limit prevents installation or corrupts memory. The module must be compiled with `-O2`, as Starlet ARM926EJ-S interworking and supervisor routines break under `-Os` size optimization (causing boot black screens). The planned strategy, when more features are added, is to offload processing to the RP2040 adapter rather than growing the console module: the adapter has ample RAM and CPU power, while the console module remains strictly dedicated to host routing and audio transport within its 36 KiB ceiling.

**Strict two-controller limit and shared polling budget.** The system is architecturally and physically bounded to a maximum of two (2) DualShock 4 controllers (`MAX_FAKE_WIIMOTES = 2` in the cIOS module, `USB_OUTPUT_PORTS = 2` in the adapter firmware). A third controller cannot connect: once two controllers are active, the adapter disables Bluetooth page scan (`gap_connectable_control(0)`), completely ignoring any additional connection requests.

This limit is dictated by both USB and Bluetooth throughput constraints:
1. **USB polling budget:** The console module handles incoming reports from the adapter at an effective delivery of up to two hundred reports per second across the shared USB endpoint. With one controller connected, that controller receives the full bandwidth; with two controllers connected at once, reports alternate by report identifier (0x01 for Player 1, 0x02 for Player 2) and share that throughput, so each one receives about half of the total rate. Both controllers remain independent and correct, but the per-player sample rate is lower in two-player sessions than in single-player ones.
2. **Bluetooth radio airtime and audio:** The RP2040 CYW43 Bluetooth radio must service concurrent motion report polling (at ~250 Hz from each controller) alongside real-time 16 kHz SBC encoded audio streaming. Adding a third or fourth controller over the same radio would exhaust airtime slots, resulting in dropped motion samples and audio crackling.

**The comparison standard.** Everything in this project is judged against the same standard: the real hardware, in the same game, doing the same gesture. Where the project and the real remote match, that is stated with the measurement. Where they differ and cannot be made to match, that is stated here. Nothing in this project is designed to be good enough by feel alone: the feeling is checked against the measurement, and the measurement is checked against the real remote.
